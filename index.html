Context

Forward logistics is a line. Reverse logistics is a fork.

Getting a product to a customer has one destination. Getting it back has four, and no one knows which until someone looks at the item. That single asymmetry explains almost every cost in this case study.

SimplySending a toy to a shop is easy — it has one address. When the toy comes back, someone has to open the box and decide if it can be sold again, fixed, melted down, or thrown away. Deciding is the hard part.
Forwardpredictable, batched, one outcome
Seller Warehouse Courier Customer

Volume is forecast. Items are identical, boxed, and known-good. Cost per unit falls as scale rises.

Reverseunpredictable, one-by-one, four outcomes
Customer Pickup Return hub Inspect One of four fates

Volume is a surprise. Every item arrives in a different condition, so every item needs a judgement. Cost per unit stays stubbornly flat no matter how big you get — which is exactly why it is worth designing.

What breaks

Four pressures pulling in opposite directions

Any returns design that relieves one of these usually worsens another. Speeding up refunds invites fraud. Screening harder punishes honest customers. This tension is the actual product problem.

SimplyShops want to be quick and kind to customers, but also careful about cheating, cheap to run, and not wasteful. You cannot be best at all four at once, so you have to choose carefully.
~$30

Cost: a return is worth less than zero

Processing runs roughly $30 per $100 returned once you stack reverse freight, handling, inspection, repackaging and markdown. On top of that, the original sale reverses. Apparel processing lands around $25–35 per item.

Optoro / Pitney Bowes benchmarks, 2026 cost-per-item analysis
19.3%

Delay: online returns run 2–3× in-store

Almost one in five online orders comes back, against roughly 8.7% in physical stores. Volume surges after sale events, and a manual inspection queue is the bottleneck that turns a 2-day refund promise into a 9-day one.

NRF & Happy Returns, 2025 Retail Returns Landscape
9%

Control: fraud is real, and over-policing is worse

Around 9% of 2025 returns were classified as fraudulent, and 45% of consumers say bending the truth on a return is acceptable. But 71% will avoid a retailer after one bad returns experience — so the cost of wrongly flagging an honest customer is not symmetric.

Climate: the fourth fate is a landfill

An estimated 9.5 billion pounds of returned goods reach US landfills each year. Every item wrongly routed to scrap that could have been restocked is both a financial write-off and an environmental one. The same decision governs both.

Scoping

What I deliberately refused to build

A first release is defined by its exclusions. Each of these is excluded for a stated reason, not because I ran out of time.

In the first release

  • Eligible, photo-supported customer returns
  • One narrow category: women's apparel
  • One market, one currency, one policy
  • A recommended route, plus a recommended item disposition
  • Prepaid and COD orders, treated differently

Out — and why

  • Final fraud adjudication. Accusing a customer is a legal and brand decision, not a scoring decision.
  • Fully automatic refunds. Money movement without a reversible step is not worth the blast radius in v1.
  • Cross-border returns. Duties, reconsignment and consumer-rights regimes differ by country; a single rules engine would quietly get one of them wrong.

Later, if evidence supports it

  • Electronics and home, where defects are functional rather than visual
  • A second market, once the first has a clean baseline
  • Preventing returns upstream — sizing, fit, listing accuracy

On the word "global": my research was global, my MVP is not — on purpose. India runs roughly 17% returns overall but 25–40% in fashion, with COD return-to-origin between 20% and 40%, against under 15% for prepaid. The US online rate sits near 19.3%. A system tuned on prepaid US apparel would misread an Indian COD festive-season return entirely. Knowing that is the reason to start in one market, not the reason to skip the research.

The product

Two decisions, one photo

Most returns tools answer "does this customer get their money?" That leaves the expensive half unanswered: what happens to the item? This prototype answers both from the same evidence — and shows its reasoning rather than just its verdict.

SimplyPick one of the four returned things below and press the button. The tool tells you two things: what the customer is told, and what happens to the item. Try the blurry one — it deliberately refuses to guess.

    How it would work

    Rules first, vision second, human last

    The order matters. Deterministic policy checks run before any model sees anything, so an ineligible return is rejected by a rule, not by a recommendation. Vision only ever describes; it never decides.

    SimplyFirst the computer checks the simple rules, like "is it still within 30 days?" Then it looks at the photo and describes what it sees. Then, if anything is unclear, a real person decides.

    Request

    Customer picks the order and reason, uploads a photo with consent, chooses a pickup slot. The screen states what happens next and how long it takes.

    ›

    Rules, then vision

    Return window, SKU eligibility and payment mode are checked deterministically. Only then does a vision API return a bounded, structured description of visible condition. Unclear evidence is surfaced, not smoothed over.

    ›

    Two recommendations

    A customer route and an item disposition, each with the reason attached and both logged. Reviewers can override either one; the override is the training signal.

    What this is not This is a concept and a clickable prototype. No custom model was trained, no labelled dataset was built, no retailer pilot was run, and no accuracy or savings figure is claimed. The score bands above are design guardrails for a conversation with an operations team, not measured thresholds. Stating this plainly is part of the work — a portfolio piece that quietly implies a deployed system fails the first honest question in an interview.
    Trade-offs

    Three choices, three prices paid

    Each of these buys something and costs something. Naming the cost is what separates a decision from a preference.

    Keep humans in the middle band

    Scores between 31 and 65 always reach a trained reviewer. This is where context, exceptions and appeals live, and where a wrong automated call does the most brand damage.

    PriceHandling time, headcount, reviewer training, queue visibility and an override log. The middle band is the expensive band by design.

    Deterministic rules before anything else

    Return windows, SKU eligibility and payment mode are code, not judgement. A customer can be shown the exact clause that governed their outcome.

    PricePolicy changes need version control and regression tests, and rules alone cannot assess whether a hem is torn or merely creased.

    Vision as a describer, never a judge

    An existing vision API converts a photo into structured observations that feed a recommendation. No custom model, no training set, no accuracy claim.

    PriceImage quality varies wildly, vendor behaviour can drift between versions, and per-call cost scales with volume. Poor photos must route to a person, not to a guess.

    Design contribution

    The customer never sees a score

    All of the machinery above surfaces to a customer as three things: a clear reason, a visible position in the journey, and a way to disagree. Click the tracker to advance it.

    Show the reason, not the algorithm

    The card names the specific policy clause that produced the outcome. A customer who understands the reason argues with the policy; a customer who sees only a verdict argues with your support team. One of those is a product problem you can fix.

    Make the reverse journey visible

    Forward orders get live tracking; returns usually get silence, and silence generates the "where is my refund" ticket. Pickup → hub → checked → refund is four states, and showing them removes the single most common contact reason in returns support.

    Put the appeal on the same screen as the decision

    An appeal route buried in a help centre is a decision with no appeal. Placing it directly under the outcome is a deliberate cost: more appeals, but appeals are a measurable quality signal and a silent, angry customer is not.

    The print-on-demand edge case

    A personalised or printed item has no resale value at all — for POD the disposition fork collapses from four outcomes to two, recycle or scrap, and a restock recommendation would be actively wrong. The honest product answer is not smarter triage but a different policy: exchange and reprint by default, refund by exception. It is the clearest example in this project of a case where the right move is to change the policy rather than automate it.

    Ownership

    Six people who can kill this

    Each has a veto. Knowing what they would ask in the first meeting is most of the job.

    Customer

    Wants a clear policy, predictable timing and a route to disagree.

    "Why was I refused?"

    Returns operations

    Owns the review queue, disposition instructions and override consistency.

    "What does my queue look like on day one?"

    Customer support

    Needs explanation scripts, escalation paths and early sight of complaint spikes.

    "What do I say when the customer disagrees?"

    Finance & merchandising

    Owns refund rules, the cost baseline and recovery rate on returned stock.

    "What is the baseline I am being measured against?"

    Legal & privacy

    Owns policy transparency, photo retention, deletion and consumer-rights review.

    "How long do we keep customer photographs?"

    Data & engineering

    Owns data quality, logging, evaluation, reliability and a rollback path.

    "How do we turn it off in an hour?"
    Responsible design

    Four commitments that cost something

    Framed by the NIST AI Risk Management Framework's emphasis on documentation, human oversight, fairness and monitoring. Each one makes the system slower or more expensive, which is how you can tell it is real.

    Human escalation with a written reason

    Low-confidence, disputed or sensitive cases route to a trained reviewer, and the reason for escalation is recorded. "The model was unsure" is not a reason; "photo showed only packaging" is.

    Appeals as a first-class path

    Every outcome shows the governing policy clause and a way to challenge it, on the same screen. Appeal volume is monitored as a quality metric, not as a nuisance.

    Privacy by design

    Collect only what the return requires. Retention, access and deletion rules for customer photographs are agreed before launch, not retrofitted after the first complaint.

    Fairness monitoring on overrides

    Overrides, appeals and error patterns are reviewed across customer segments. If one group is escalated disproportionately, that is a defect, and it is treated like one.

    Appendix

    The questions I expect

    Prepared answers to the nine questions this project invites — including the four uncomfortable ones.

    Why this project

    Everyone talks about how to sell. Very few talk about what happens after.

    I chose reverse logistics because it sits exactly where operations, customer trust and sustainability collide — which means it cannot be reasoned about from one function alone. It is also under-explored enough that I had to build my own structure rather than borrow someone else's.

    I started with no prior project experience and no team. What I had to produce myself was the research, the framing, the framework, the scoping decisions, the prototype and the argument for all of it.

    What this demonstrates

    Narrowing. I mapped six stages and built one. The discipline to cut scope is visible in the exclusions, each with a stated reason.

    Structure under ambiguity. The 4C lens is mine. It exists because an unstructured topic needed a way to be interrogated rather than described.

    Intellectual honesty. Every claim is labelled sourced, my own, or untested. I would rather be trusted on a smaller claim than doubted on a larger one.

    Communication. From a landfill statistic to a clickable console to a plain-language toggle — the same argument, pitched at whoever is reading.

    Where my internships connect: business development and campus ambassador work taught me that trust is built at the awkward moments, not the easy ones. In e-commerce, the return is the awkward moment.
